I. Our name

Resham and naama

Resham is silk, and a naama is a written account, the kind of chronicle that records a life one entry at a time. Every saree we weave is an account of its own: the weaver, the months on the loom and every crossing of warp and weft are written into the cloth, thread by thread.

The warp

Fine mulberry silk, dyed in our own vats and wound onto the warp beam by hand. It sets the length of the saree and the discipline of the whole cloth.

The weft

Thrown by shuttle through the opened shed, pick after pick, and beaten into place with the reed. The weaver’s rhythm lives in the weft, and you can feel it in the hand of the finished cloth.

The zari

Real zari is fine silver wire, flattened and wound around a silk core, then gilded. It is laid in by hand as an extra weft, and that is what makes a motif rise from the ground like light on water.

IV. The craft

Five stages, and not one of them can be hurried.

A Banarasi saree passes through many pairs of hands before it reaches yours. Each stage below happens in the lanes around our studio, within walking distance of the loom where your saree will be woven.

  1. i

    Naksha

    The design is drawn square by square on graph paper by a naksha-maker, and every square decides whether a warp thread lifts or stays down.

  2. ii

    Pattern cards

    The naksha is punched into a chain of jacquard cards, one card for every pick of the motif, and laced above the pit loom.

  3. iii

    Warp

    Several thousand ends of silk are dyed, sized, stretched along the lane and drawn one by one through the heddles and the reed.

  4. iv

    Weaving

    The weaver throws the ground weft with the shuttle and lays in the zari by hand from small spools, often with a second weaver at the loom for the heaviest pieces.

  5. v

    Finishing

    The cloth is checked in daylight, pressed by hand and folded. The weaver’s name is already there, woven into the end of the pallu.

The reverse

The back of a saree tells you how it was woven.

Two sarees can look alike from the front and be made in very different ways. Turn the cloth over and the difference is plain to see, which is why we invite every client to do it before they buy.

Face

Kadwa

Each motif is woven with its own length of zari, turned back at the edge of the flower. Nothing crosses the back, nothing is cut, and the motif cannot pull loose. It is slow, and it is how we weave every buti.

Face

Fekwa

The zari is thrown from selvedge to selvedge and the floats between motifs are cut away by hand once the cloth is off the loom. It is quicker and lighter, and the cut ends show on the reverse.

A weaver’s vocabulary

Katan
Pure silk with the filaments twisted into a firm thread, woven plain. The classic ground of a Banarasi saree.
Zari
Metallic thread for brocade. Real zari is silver, flattened and wound around silk, then gilded, or left bright as silver zari against gold-toned silks.
Kadwa
Each motif woven separately with its own extra weft, so the reverse stays clean and nothing is cut.
Fekwa
Extra weft carried across the full width, with the floats between motifs cut away after weaving.
Jangla
A dense all-over jaal of creepers and flowers, named for the wild growth of the jungle.
Meenakari
Coloured silk extra weft set within zari motifs, borrowed in spirit from enamel work on gold.
Tanchoi
A weft-figured silk worked in several coloured wefts on a satin ground, usually without zari.
Dhoop chhaon
Sunshine and shade: warp and weft in different colours, so the silk turns from one to the other.
Buti, buta, konia
A small scattered motif, its larger cousin, and the corner motif where the border meets the pallu.

V. The cooperative

A fair price, divided in the open.

A handwoven Banarasi costs what it does because of time: weeks of a master weaver’s hands, silver in the zari, and silk dyed with patience. We publish how the price of every saree is shared, so you can see where your money goes before you spend it.

  • The master weaver’s name, woven into the end of the pallu.
  • A handwritten loom card stating the days the saree spent on the loom.
  • Real silver zari, with a written note of its silver content.
  • Free repairs to the weave for as long as the saree is worn.
